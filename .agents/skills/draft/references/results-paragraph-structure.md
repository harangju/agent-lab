# Results Paragraph Structure

Use this for empirical Results (and parallel theory-illustration) paragraphs. One paragraph, one question. The reader should know what is being asked before numbers arrive, and what the numbers mean before the next analysis starts.

## Sequence

1. Signpost the question.
   - Topic sentence narrates the turn: why this test now, what it answers.
   - Prefer stems like "To test whether...", "We next ask...", "To explain why X holds, we examined Y."

2. Design or sample cue.
   - One short clause or sentence on who/what/when or the contrast being estimated.
   - Only what is needed to read the finding; full design lives in Methods.

3. Finding.
   - State the result in plain language first.
   - Then give the key statistic, estimate, or figure pointer.
   - Lead with the claim-bearing noun; keep the subject short.

4. Interpretation.
   - Final sentence says what the evidence means for the paper's claim.
   - Motivate the next analysis only if the bridge is real; do not invent a new term just to set up the next paragraph.

## Practical Compression

When space is tight:

1. Question.
2. Finding (+ number).
3. Meaning.

Fold the design cue into the question or finding sentence.

## Multi-panel / table paragraphs

- Open with the shared question the display answers.
- Walk panels or columns in parallel syntax when they are parallel tests.
- Close by interpreting the pattern across cells, not the last cell alone.

## Editing Checks

- Do not open with "This result..." or "These findings..." that force a lookup to the prior paragraph.
- Do not end on a raw number with no interpretation.
- Do not drop a new analysis cold without a signpost.
- Keep claim strength honest: illustration ≠ identification ≠ proof.
- One topic per paragraph; split when a second question appears.

## Whole Results section

Use this when drafting a full Results section rather than single paragraphs.

### Macro arc (what, so what, how, why)

1. Order subsections to build a causal narrative:
   - **What:** present the outcomes (what happened).
   - **So what:** validate them (does the effect matter in the real world?).
   - **How:** describe the process differences.
   - **Why:** link process to outcomes.
2. Within each phase, order findings by increasing novelty: confirmed or intuitive effects first, mixed effects second, novel or counterintuitive effects last.
3. Make transitions between subsections explicit: "Our findings on X raise a question about Y." "Having established X, we now examine Y." "To explain why X, we Y."

### Claim headers

- State each subsection's finding as a claim in its bold header: "**Collaborating with AI agents increases individual-level productivity.**" A reader skimming only the headers should get the paper's story.
- A header makes a claim; it doesn't name a topic. Write "X increases Y", not "Effects of X on Y".

### Synthesizing paragraph

After two or three related subsections, write one paragraph that opens with "Taken together, ..." and combines their findings into a single interpretive claim. Name the pattern in words the reader already knows rather than coining a label.

### Section check

Read all the claim headers in sequence. Do they tell the paper's story, and does the narrative build to the core contribution?
