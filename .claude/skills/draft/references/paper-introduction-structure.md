# Paper Introduction Structure

Use this for drafting or revising a scholarly introduction. The goal is an hourglass: start broad enough for a nearby field, narrow to a concrete gap, then widen into the paper's contribution and roadmap. Do not copy the abstract's opening subject or framing (see project STYLE rule on distinct openings).

## Sequence (CARS / hourglass)

1. Field setup.
   - One or two sentences.
   - Name the phenomenon or practical setting in plain language.
   - Readable to a scholar outside the immediate niche.

2. Why it matters now.
   - One or two sentences.
   - Cost, opportunity, or change that makes the problem urgent.
   - Prefer a concrete stake over "important" or "timely."

3. What we already know.
   - Short cluster of sentences, not a literature dump.
   - Group prior work by claim or approach, not by author list.
   - Leave the reader with the settled baseline the paper will move.

4. Gap or misread.
   - One clear sentence (sometimes two).
   - State what is unknown, unresolved, conflated, or untested.
   - The contribution should feel necessary once this lands.

5. This paper.
   - One sentence that names the contribution in concrete terms.
   - Prefer "This paper..." / "We..." over vague "we explore."
   - Match the claim strength to the evidence later in the manuscript.

6. Approach cue.
   - One or two sentences on design, setting, or theory instrument.
   - Enough to make the contribution believable; methods detail belongs later.

7. What we find / claim (preview).
   - One to three sentences of the main result or theoretical payoff.
   - Do not paste the full results section. Preview the punchline only.

8. Roadmap.
   - Brief tour of sections or studies when the paper is long or multi-part.
   - Skip or compress for short letters.

## Practical Compression

For a tight intro (common in letters and short empirics):

1. Setting + stake.
2. Baseline knowledge (compressed).
3. Gap.
4. This paper + approach.
5. Punchline preview.
6. Roadmap only if needed.

## Editing Checks

- Abstract and intro must not open on the same subject with the same framing.
- The gap sentence should make the contribution feel required, not optional.
- Related work in the intro should position, not inventory; a full related-work section can carry the catalog.
- Contribution language must not outrun the data or proofs in the body.
- Topic sentences should skim: a reader of first sentences alone should get the argument spine.
- Cut throat-clearing ("In recent years..."), taxonomy openers, and long list-subjects before the verb.
