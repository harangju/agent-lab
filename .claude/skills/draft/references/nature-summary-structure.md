# Nature-Style Summary Paragraph Structure

Use this as a structure guide for accessible scholarly abstracts and summary paragraphs. The goal is a single readable paragraph that moves from broad field context to the paper's contribution and broader importance.

## Sequence

1. Basic introduction to the field.
   - Use one or two sentences.
   - Make it comprehensible to a scientist or scholar outside the immediate field.

2. More detailed background.
   - Use two or three sentences when space permits.
   - Make it comprehensible to readers in related disciplines.
   - Explain the key concepts needed to understand the problem.

3. General problem addressed by the study.
   - Use one clear sentence.
   - State what is unknown, unresolved, misread, or missing.

4. Main result or contribution.
   - Use one sentence.
   - Use "Here I show", "Here we show", "This paper shows", or a close equivalent when appropriate.

5. What the result reveals.
   - Use two or three sentences.
   - Explain how the result changes, unifies, corrects, or adds to prior knowledge.
   - Compare directly to what was thought before when that comparison matters.

6. More general context.
   - Use one or two sentences.
   - Explain why the result matters beyond the immediate case.

7. Broader perspective.
   - Use two or three sentences only if it improves accessibility.
   - Keep it readable to non-specialists.
   - In a long summary paragraph, total length can approach 250 to 300 words, but shorter is usually better.

## Practical Compression

For a tight abstract, compress the structure into six moves:

1. Field.
2. Gap.
3. Here we/I contribution.
4. What it shows.
5. Evidence.
6. Broader implication.

## Editing Checks

- The first sentence should not require the reader to know the manuscript's specialized vocabulary.
- The gap sentence should make the contribution feel necessary.
- The contribution sentence should be concrete enough that the reader knows what the paper does.
- The evidence sentence should distinguish illustration from proof.
- The final sentence should widen the lens without overclaiming.

## Before restructuring an abstract

1. Read the current abstract, plus enough of the surrounding text to understand the paper's actual claims, structure, evidence, and limitations.
2. Identify the abstract's job. It might need to summarize the paper, sell the contribution, match a target journal's style, or make the manuscript easier to enter.
3. Tighten aggressively. Remove duplicate framing, long inventories, throat-clearing, and technical details the body can carry.
4. Preserve the manuscript's actual claims. Don't upgrade suggestive evidence into proof, and don't introduce concepts the paper doesn't contain.

## Revision heuristics

- If the abstract opens with a taxonomy, replace it with the general problem that makes the taxonomy matter.
- If a long list comes before the verb, compress the list into a category.
- If the abstract keeps repeating its core object, define it once and move on.
- If the abstract is technical, add one accessible sentence before the notation.
- If the empirical part feels bolted on, add one clean transition before the findings.
- If the final sentence is narrow, replace it with the paper's most general implication.
